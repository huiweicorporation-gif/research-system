CLAIM CHECK (primary sources searched 2026-11-02)
Status: SOLID = primary source agrees; DEBATED = primary source exists but is disputed or conflicting;
WEAK = only secondary or anecdotal sources; say it as a hypothesis or cut it.

1. Hook: "many hunter-gatherer groups barely add salt"                                  -> DEBATED, rewrite
   - SOLID: Yanomami (Amazon), INTERSALT 1988: mean urinary sodium 0.9 mmol/24 h, under 1 mmol in 84% of
     195 people, the lowest of all 52 INTERSALT centres (most others ~150 mmol).
     Sources: INTERSALT (BMJ 1988); Yanomamo BP study, PubMed 2810327; BMJ 1988;297:319 commentary.
   - BUT the Yanomami are foraging horticulturalists (plantains and bananas give most calories), not strict
     hunter-gatherers. Do not call them hunter-gatherers.
   - WEAK: Cameroon Pygmy hunter-gatherers show low urinary sodium (one study); Hadza "no added salt" only in
     a blog post. Not enough for "many groups".
   - Eaton & Konner, NEJM 1985;312:283-9 estimate a low-sodium Paleolithic diet, but it is a model, the exact
     figure was not confirmed from the primary text, and the paleo reconstruction is criticised.
   Use: "In one Amazon community, scientists measured the lowest salt intake ever recorded..." Name the
   measurement, not a general rule about ancestors.

2. "Hunter-gatherers got sodium from meat, blood and organs"                             -> WEAK
   Animal foods contain sodium (general fact, safe). The Hadza details are from a blog post only.
   Use as a plain statement of fact about animal foods; no specific tribe claim.

3. "Farming raised the need for salt"                                                    -> WEAK (hypothesis)
   Bunge's 19th-century potassium idea: plant-heavy diets bring potassium that the body sheds with sodium,
   so plant eaters seek salt. Found only secondhand (1911 newspaper quoting Abderhalden); counter-examples
   exist (USGS beaver study). Weller (Documenta Praehistorica 2015) treats the Neolithic-diet explanation as
   a nutritionists' hypothesis still being tested against archaeology.
   Use: one of the competing guesses, said as a guess. Never "farming made us need salt".

4. "Salt appetite is an ancient instinct"                                                -> SOLID
   Denton and colleagues: salt appetite is an innate drive shaped over 100+ million years in land animals;
   sodium depletion raises the reward value of salt (PNAS 2011; Nature 2019; Nutrients 2023 review).
   This is the safest backbone of the episode.

5. Earliest salt making                                                                 -> DEBATED, give a range
   - Poiana Slatinei-Lunca, Romania: brine exploitation c. 6050-5500 BC, earliest known in Europe
     (Weller & Dumitroaia, Antiquity 2005; supported by Dufraisse et al. 2012). The same 2005 report also has
     two radiocarbon dates of 1750-1630 BC. Say "about 8,000 years ago, at the earliest known site in Europe".
   - Zhongba, Chongqing, China: brine boiling in the first millennium BC with chemical evidence
     (Flad et al., PNAS 2005;102:12618). Do not call it the earliest in China.
   - "Briquetage" as a term only appears in secondary sources found so far; describe the pottery instead.

6. "Paleolithic potassium was 3-5x today's"                                              -> WEAK
   Only in a secondary abstract citing Eaton & Konner 1985. Cut, or say "much higher, by some estimates".
