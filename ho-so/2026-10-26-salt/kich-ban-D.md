## 1. Lời đọc video dài (tiếng Anh)

You open a bag of chips. You tell yourself: just one. Ten minutes later your fingers are touching the bottom of
the bag, and there's a fine white dust on your thumb. You didn't decide that. Something older than you did.

So here's the question. Why does your body crave salt?

It sounds like a silly question, until you learn this. Scientists once measured how much salt people eat in
fifty-two communities around the world. In one of them, deep in the Amazon rainforest, people were eating almost
none. And they were living their lives just fine. [1]

So if humans can live on almost no added salt, why does a single potato chip feel so impossible to stop at?

Researchers have found three clues. The first one shows how little salt humans can live on. The second one shows
when we started making it. And the third one goes back further than humans, back to a time when dinosaurs still
walked the Earth, about a hundred million years. That's the one that explains you.

Clue one. The people who barely use salt.

In the 1980s, a huge study called INTERSALT set out to measure salt across the planet. [1] Researchers visited
communities from cities in Europe and North America to villages in Africa, Asia and South America. They didn't
ask people what they ate. People forget, and people round down. Instead they did something more honest. They
collected everything each person passed in a full day, and measured the sodium in it. Sodium is the part of salt
your body actually uses.

In most places, the numbers looked familiar. Around a hundred and fifty units a day, give or take.

Then the team reached the Yanomami, a people who live in villages in the rainforest on the border of Brazil and
Venezuela. Their gardens grow plantains and bananas. The men hunt and fish. The women tend the crops. There is no
salt shaker on the table, because there is no salt on the table. [2]

Their number was not a hundred and fifty. It was not fifty. It was less than one. [1]

Most of the people tested were below that line. It was the lowest salt intake the study recorded anywhere on
Earth.

And the Yanomami were not alone. A handful of other communities in the study also came in far below everyone
else: the Xingu people, also in the Brazilian rainforest, a community in Papua New Guinea, and one in rural Kenya. [1]
Four places, on three continents, where people lived on a small fraction of the salt most of the world eats.

That made the scientists' job harder, not easier. These four places were so different from the rest that the
main analysis of the study left them out of some of its comparisons. And researchers still argue about what
exactly those four numbers tell us. [1] But nobody argues about the numbers themselves. People can live on very,
very little added salt.

So where does their sodium come from? From the food itself. Meat, fish and other animal foods carry a little
sodium naturally, and the body is very good at holding on to it. [3] You don't need a salt mine to stay alive.
For most of human history, nobody had one.

Think about what that means for a moment. For hundreds of thousands of years, our ancestors walked, hunted,
gathered, raised children and told stories around fires, and there was no salt shaker anywhere. No bags of
chips. No soy sauce. No bacon. Whatever sodium they had came hidden inside their food, a little at a time.

But that only makes the mystery bigger. If the body can manage on so little, why does it want so much?

Clue two. The first salt works.

Picture a cold valley in what is now northeastern Romania, about eight thousand years ago. A spring bubbles out
of the ground. The water tastes strange, sharp, almost bitter. Early farmers have noticed it. And they've started
doing something with it.

They carry the salty water to fires. They pour it into clay pots and boil it, again and again, until a crust of
white crystals is left behind. Archaeologists working at a site called Poiana Slatinei have found the evidence:
thick layers of ash and charcoal, and broken pottery, piled up over generations. [4]

It's the earliest known salt making in Europe. And it was done by farmers, not by hunters.

Picture how much work that was. Hauling water. Gathering wood for the fires, day after day. Making the pots,
then breaking them to get the salt out. You don't do that for something you can live without. You do that for
something you want very, very badly.

Thousands of years later, on the other side of the world, the same idea shows up again. At a site called Zhongba,
in central China, people were boiling salty water on a much larger scale, filling pot after pot. Scientists
tested the residue inside those pots, and found the chemical traces of salt making. [5]

Notice who keeps showing up at these salt works. Farmers. Villages. Settled people.

Why would farmers suddenly need salt? Nobody knows for sure. One old idea, first suggested in the eighteen
hundreds, is about plants. A diet heavy in plants is rich in another mineral, potassium, and some researchers
think that pushes the body to seek more sodium to balance it. [6] It's a guess. It has been argued about for
more than a century, and the evidence is still being tested against what archaeologists dig up. [7]

Other researchers point to something simpler. Salt keeps food from spoiling. A village with a harvest to store
has a reason to want salt that a family on the move does not.

Whatever the reason, humans did not invent the hunger for salt in those villages. They only found a way to feed
it. The hunger itself is much, much older.

Clue three. The instinct older than humans.

Leave the village. Walk into a forest anywhere on Earth, and wait near the right patch of ground. A patch where
the soil is a little salty.

Deer come. Then goats, or elephants, or monkeys, depending on where you are. They lick the dirt. They chew the
clay. Some of them walk a long way to get there. Scientists call these places salt licks. [8]

But first, why does any animal need sodium at all? Because almost everything your body does runs on it. Every
time a nerve fires, every time a muscle tightens, every beat of your heart, tiny gates in your cells open and let
sodium flow through. Sodium also helps the body decide where water goes. So sodium is not a luxury. It's part of
the wiring. [10]

The problem is getting it.

Now think about why animals go looking for it. In the ocean, sodium is everywhere. Every fish swims in it. But when animals crawled out onto
land, they left that endless supply behind. On land, sodium is hard to find. Rain washes it out of the soil.
Plants barely need it, so they barely hold it.

So land animals evolved a solution. A drive. A built-in hunger for salt that switches on when the body runs low.
Scientists who have studied this for decades believe it took shape over more than a hundred million years. [8]

One of them, an Australian scientist named Derek Denton, spent much of his career on this one question. His
conclusion was simple. Salt hunger is not a habit animals pick up. It's an instinct, as basic as thirst. And
many land animals, including early humans, probably spent much of their lives just a little short of sodium. [8]
Not starving. Just never quite sure where the next salt would come from.

And here is the strangest part. When an animal is short on sodium, salt doesn't just seem useful. It tastes
better. Experiments show the brain turns up the reward. The same salt that tasted ordinary yesterday suddenly
feels delicious. [9]

Your tongue is part of this too. It has its own way of detecting salt, a dedicated sense, separate from sweet or
sour or bitter. [9] Evolution doesn't build a special sensor for something that doesn't matter. It built one for
salt because finding salt was a question of staying alive.

You carry that same machinery. The craving you feel at the bottom of a bag of chips is not a flaw. It's a piece
of survival software, written for a world where salt was rare and precious, and an animal that found it lived a
little longer than one that didn't.

So, why does your body crave salt?

Because for almost all of its history, your body could never be sure it would find any. The Yanomami show how
little a human can live on. The first salt works show how hard our ancestors worked to get more. And the hundred
million years before that explain why it feels so good when you do.

The world around you has changed. The salt is everywhere now. But the instinct is still running the old program.

Here's my question for you. What's the one salty food you could never give up? Tell me in the comments.

And if you like finding out why your body does the strange things it does, subscribe. Next time, another "why do
humans" story.

## 4. Điểm cấm đã soát (kiem-duyet: rủi ro trung-binh, sức khỏe)

- Hình người Yanomami chỉ dùng tranh minh hoạ, tả tôn trọng (làng, vườn, săn bắn); không dùng ảnh thật, không
  dùng từ "tribe" hay "primitive".
- Không dùng ảnh khai quật có bản quyền; vẽ lại hố khảo cổ, gốm, than.
