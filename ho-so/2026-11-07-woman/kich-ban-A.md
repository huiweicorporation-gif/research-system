## 1. Lời đọc video dài (tiếng Anh)

Your grandmother stopped having babies around fifty, then lived for decades. Almost every other animal dies soon
after. Why? [1]

Think about how strange that is. In most animals, females keep having young until close to the end of their lives.
When the body stops making babies, it usually doesn't have long left.

Humans are different. Twenty more years. Thirty. Sometimes forty.

From the cold point of view of evolution, that looks like a mistake. Evolution counts children. A body that stops
having them should stop being worth keeping. So why does it keep going?

Scientists have found three clues. The first one shows this isn't just modern medicine. The second one comes from
the ocean. And the third one comes from a group of women digging in the dry hills of Tanzania. That's the one that
explains your grandmother. But it's also the one scientists argue about most.

Clue one. It isn't just hospitals.

The easy answer is that old age is new. People used to die young, the story goes, and only doctors and clean water
gave us these extra years.

That story is half true. In the past, many people did die young, mostly as children. But researchers who studied
mortality records from hunter-gatherer and small farming peoples around the world found something else. [2] Once
people got through childhood, many of them lived well past their fifties and sixties. The researchers estimated a
natural human lifespan of roughly seventy years, in societies with no hospitals at all. [2]

So the long life after children isn't a side effect of the modern world. It looks like part of what a human is.

And that makes the puzzle sharper. If it's built in, something must have built it.

Clue two. The whales.

For a long time, scientists thought humans were alone in this. Then they started looking at the sea.

Off the coasts of Canada and the United States, a few groups of killer whales have been watched closely for more
than forty years. [3] Researchers know each animal by the shape of its fin. They know who is whose mother. And they
noticed that female killer whales stop having calves long before they die, and then keep living for decades.

But you can't follow every whale in the ocean for forty years. So for other species, scientists tried something
else. They looked inside. When whales wash up on beaches, researchers can study their ovaries, the organs that
hold a female's eggs. An ovary keeps a record. It shows whether a female was still breeding when she died, or had
stopped long before. In 2018, a team went through these records for sixteen kinds of toothed whales. In most of
them, old females were still breeding. In three, they weren't. [4]

Since then, scientists have found the same pattern in a handful of other toothed whales. Short-finned pilot whales.
Belugas. Narwhals. False killer whales. [4] Together with humans, that makes six species we know of. Out of
thousands of mammals. A study in 2024 found that the females of these whales live about forty years longer than
females of other whales their size. [5] Their bodies didn't stop breeding early. Their lives got longer, and the
breeding didn't follow.

So what are these old whales doing with all that time?

The killer whales eat salmon. And salmon is not always easy to find. Some years there's plenty. Some years there's
almost none. When researchers studied video of the whales moving through their feeding grounds, they found that the
old females, the ones past breeding, were often the ones in front. [6] They led the group. And they led most in the
hard years, when salmon was scarce. The old females seemed to remember where food could be found when the usual
places were empty.

Then came a bigger test. Researchers went through thirty-six years of records on two killer whale populations and
asked a simple question. Do young whales with a grandmother alive do better than young whales without one? [7]

They do. And the grandmothers who had stopped having calves of their own helped the most. Again, most of all in
years when food was short. [7]

There's one more twist. When an older mother and her daughter both had calves at the same time, the older mother's
calf was more likely to die. [8] In a family that shares its fish, a mother and daughter breeding together were
competing. Stopping, it seems, avoided that fight, and freed the grandmother to help.

So in whales, at least, the old females aren't just hanging around. They might be the reason the family survives.

Which brings us back to humans.

Clue three. The women with the digging sticks.

In the 1980s, a team led by the anthropologist Kristen Hawkes lived with the Hadza, a people in northern Tanzania
who still found most of their food in the wild. [9] The team wanted to know who did the work of feeding children.

What surprised them were the older women. Women past their child-bearing years were some of the hardest workers in
camp. They spent long hours digging tubers, deep roots that are hard to pull out of the rocky ground. [9]

Think about what that means. A young child can pick a berry off a bush. But a child can't dig a root out of hard
ground with a stick. That takes strength, skill and patience. So a child who depends on roots depends on an adult.
And the question is: which adult?

If you're a woman with no young children of your own, there's no one you have to carry, and no one you have to
nurse. All the food you dig can go to someone else. In a camp where everyone is related, that someone is very often
your daughter's child.

Then the team weighed the children. When a mother had a new baby to nurse, she had less time to dig. Her older
children's weight then depended less on her work, and more on how much their grandmother was gathering. [10]

From this, Hawkes and her colleagues built an idea that became known as the grandmother hypothesis. [11] It goes
like this. Long ago, our ancestors moved into places where children couldn't easily feed themselves. A grandmother
who kept gathering food could feed her grandchildren. Her daughter could then have her next baby sooner. Over many
generations, women who stayed strong and lived long after their last child left more descendants. And so, slowly,
evolution made long lives after fifty. [11]

The idea doesn't rest on the Hadza alone. Researchers in Finland and Canada went through old family records, going
back centuries, from before modern medicine. They found that women who lived longer after their last child had more
grandchildren. [12] Their children started families earlier and raised more of them successfully.

So, case closed? Not quite.

Here's where scientists disagree.

First, the Hadza are one group of people. Not every hunter-gatherer society looks like them. In some, like the Ache
of Paraguay, grandmothers seem to matter less. [13]

Second, some researchers built computer models of human evolution and found that grandmothers' help, on its own,
was too small to add many years to our lives. [14] Hawkes and her colleagues answered that the result came from how
the model was built. [15] That argument is still going.

And third, we may not be quite as alone as we thought. In 2023, scientists who had followed a community of wild
chimpanzees in Uganda for over twenty years reported that some females there stopped breeding and lived on. [16]
Their hormones changed the way human hormones do at menopause. None of them gave birth after fifty. But these
chimpanzees live in an unusually safe forest with plenty of food. The researchers aren't sure yet whether this is
something chimpanzees evolved, or something that only happens when life is easy. [16]

So the grandmother hypothesis is not a settled fact. It's the strongest idea we have, with real evidence behind it,
and real holes in it too. Maybe the truth is a mix. Grandmothers who feed. Grandmothers who remember. And families
that do better when mothers and daughters aren't competing.

But look at what all three clues share. The people from the hunter-gatherer records. The old whale at the front of
the group in a hungry year. The woman with the digging stick feeding a child who isn't hers.

In each case, the years after fifty aren't empty. They're filled with someone else.

For most animals, life is about having your own young. For a handful of species, it turned into something else:
helping your young have theirs.

So the next time your grandmother tells you that old story, remember. A body that stopped having children and kept
going isn't a mistake of evolution. It might be one of its oldest inventions.

What did your grandmother do that no one else in your family could? Tell me in the comments.

## 2. Lời đọc 2 Shorts (tiếng Anh, mỗi bản ~40–50 giây; làm sau bản dài)

**Short 1 · "A handful of species outlive their fertility. Why?"**

Most animals keep having babies until they're close to death. But a handful of species don't. Humans. Killer
whales. Pilot whales. Belugas. Narwhals. False killer whales. [4] Their females stop breeding and then live for
decades. In killer whales, the old females lead the group to food in hungry years, [6] and calves with a living
grandmother survive better. [7] Scientists think something similar may explain human grandmothers. But they're
still arguing about it. The full story is on the channel.

**Short 2 · "The grandmother who digs for you"**

In northern Tanzania, researchers studying the Hadza noticed something. Older women, past having children, were
some of the hardest workers in camp, digging deep roots for hours. [9] When a mother had a new baby, her older
kids' weight depended more on their grandmother's digging. [10] That's one group of people, and scientists still
debate what it means. But it gave us the grandmother hypothesis. Full story on the channel.

## 4. Điểm cấm đã soát (kiem-duyet: rủi ro thấp)

- Không có lời khuyên y tế nào về mãn kinh, hormone hay tuổi thọ; chỉ nói giả thuyết tiến hoá.
- Không nói "only 3 animals"; dùng "a handful of species", "six species we know of".
- Giả thuyết không được coi là sự thật: có đoạn riêng "Here's where scientists disagree" và câu "not a settled fact".
- Người Hadza và Ache: chỉ tranh minh hoạ, tả tôn trọng (đào củ, trại); không dùng "tribe", "primitive", không ảnh thật.
- Hình: không cảnh sơ sinh cận; "a new baby to nurse" chỉ vẽ mẹ bế bé bọc vải nhìn xa. Tối đa 2 người mỗi cảnh.
